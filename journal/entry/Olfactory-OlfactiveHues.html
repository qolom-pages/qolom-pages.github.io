Olfactory: Olfactive Hues

Colours can represent different perfumes, attars, or scents and fragrances. When a perfume is listed as "purple", "green", or "brown" - we often never mean the colour of the physical product, instead it's a means of describe what colour the perfume is perceived as.

Introduction 🔗

A quick write-up on how we use colours to help represent a variety, ever growing, of perfume categories. In general, synesthesia, is the word used when we associate colours with olfactory experiences - for example, given a fresh perfume one may class it as "blue" when facets of ozone, sea, or mascline-style citrus clean facets are present. On the other hand, freshness may indicate the smell of pines and clean forest: a "green" perfume. Both equal in their representation, a fresh perfume can be a myriad of different colours and shades of colours; thus an infinite spectrum is possible. As the categories and subjective views of perfumes grow, so do colour associations and meanings.

The classic colours 🔗

Perfume has no start in recent history, more than likely, it has no start in any recorded history. When speaking about "classic" concepts, we have to talk about the wider acceptance of colour and perfume, not to when it first started to occur in the human psyche. Fougère, the mossy-wood archetype, can be considered as the first concept of colour and fragrance in an official setting - moss, fern, herbs; primarily green. 1947 is the year when perfumer Pierre Balmain release Vent Vert - the first "green" perfume. The capitalisation of the use of colours and fragrance ran abound, the advent of "blue" scents arrived, usually being fresh, clean, and aquatic.

Almost always a "men's perfume" - a blue "women's perfume" is rare and perhaps unmarketable. The flip here is that, while men had blue, women had pink - beautiful, floral, seductive. Think rose, think sparkly, think classical European feminine perfume. Green, blue, and pink - the next advent colour was brown (or perhaps orange") to represent a particular woody facet: cedar. The erosion brown perfumes is the concept of "oriental" - using single molecule to synthesise aromatic compounds a typical woody perfume would be accompanied with amber accords; essentially turning the cedar association to a more oriental theme - amber.

Ultimately, we arrived to the classic colours and their associated fragrance families: green (moss), pink (floral), blue (clean), and brown (amber).

Sensory Map 🔗

Prior to colours, olfactive sensations were split up into categories representing a smell-taste matrix, at the top being "vegetable" and the bottom "animal." The initial proposal to categorising fragrances came from Jellinek (1951), succeeding his initial plots came a flurry of near-maddening matrices with additions such as "cheesy" and grouping outliers like lavender, coniferous, and mint as a collection. To quote the paper by Zarzo and Stanton [2009] "To reach a consensus, however, seems difficult, if at all possible."
In literature, these groupings and keywords famed by Jellinek, were later superseded by The Fragrant Wheel (Ulrich Harder, 1979), however this can be classed as "incomplete" as stated by Ann-Sophie Barwich in her paper Making Sense of Smell: Classifications and Model Thinking in Olfaction Theory (page 93 + page 103)

My personal point of contention is here, while made in good faith as one can assume, Edwards developed a Fragrance Wheel in 1992 with four key outer notes: amber, floral, woody, and fresh. These notes where then divided into a handful of inner notes - 14 in total where with 1/4 being arbitrarily used as transitive overlaps between the four key outer notes. As you can see, 14 specified sub-notes is criminal to categorise all of fragrance, only because using an infinite number would be near impossible to implement in language and olfactive literature. In modern applications, big name niche and high street brands, even artisanal and pseudo-artisanal brands subconsciously adhere to Edwards' wheel, it has saturated the industry en masse that it's the near-definitive language to describe perfume categories.

It must fall in these or you push hard enough to fit the oddly shaped square into the neatly cut out square hole. In most application, Edwards' wheel stands as the definitive map and colour grading scheme to sort perfumes: when describing a perfume as "yellow" we think "citrus" and when we say "floral ambers" we think "dark pink." These colours work in a sort of spectrum where overlapping ideas will share a hue in similarity to represent the idea "a bit of this, a bit of that" and while this does effectively work to put a perfume into a sensory map, we must first acknowledge the failure - sensory maps are 2D with a 1D sort of information style, it can be put on a straight line and still make sense - a circle is in fact a 1D shape as well, so the wheel does metaphorically come back around.

To represent olfactory sensations accurately, we need a mental model with 4 properties similar to a space-time graph - a means of describing how it smells, how it feels, the memories, and aromatic characteristics.
The same "pink" "floral" perfume may read as green to some, the grassy facets of the leaves shine more, it may be a purple fragrance because the elusive memory of romance might overpower us. Sensory maps are functional and necessary to help translate concepts, ideas, and abstractions into easy receptacles but are and will always be fundamentally useless.

Purple 🔗

Ah, purple, genuinely - what? It's so consciously nonsense that it actually has meaning, it has assigned expectations and value, yet it is so buzzwordy that it hardly matters what it means. In Edwards' wheel, purple is aromatic, to my friends and close companions it means something something that is mysterious, nuanced, and evocative, to the mass produced it means violets and irises. For me, when developing a purple fragrance - when it comes to intentionally developing one, it has to be abstract, something that isn't meaningful in a logical or sensible way: for example time, historical settings, or concepts like luxury. Purple perfumes exist and do not exist, by sheer implementation of the colour it has to be a duality "this but that" and almost always cringy "the smile of a lunatic" or even pop culture "Gojo's Purple Hollow" is a great example.

In the niche community, purple fragrances exist as an outlier to usual and common categories, it's more effectively put, an exclusion and rejection of mainstream categorisation. Though, it can be said that this exclusion comes from potentially unintended consequences of deeply organic and chaotic chemical reactions that form completely novel aromas, the use of dense rose and dense jasmine (usually in the absolute forms) can develop something similar. It can also come from clashes of typically dark and typically bright notes such as tobacco mixed with bergamot - again these are examples and assumptions, the chemical science of perfumery is too far and complex to widdle down into a list of absolutes. I think it is this complexity and the result of physical molecular chaos that brings forth purple fragrances, they are beautiful when planned and Intoxicatingly special when they are spontaneous.

It would be unjust and ironic to write so much about purple fragrances when the whole concept is that it's up to the wearer and perfumer to think of its definition, it should remain in the least elusive such that it is imperative that it must be experienced to be understood.

Conclusion 🔗

Colours are a fundamentally great way to describe perfumes and olfactory experiences not just limited to perfume; wine, tea, and coffee have their own systems for this. Although flawed, they provide a means to transmitting ideas through words. While common colour systems exist, using novel approaches or system attuned to your mental psyche might be more useful to you and how you experience the world, the smell of moss doesn't always have to be green, it could also be purple or brown. The only genuine absolute is: colours are infinite and evoke an infinite sensory experience and it's almost foolish to limit these to a handful of, again, another set of infinite ideas expressed through chaotic molecular bonding of aromatic chemicals.
